## 1. Periods of English Literature

| Period                     | Years        | Key Features / Writers                       |
| -------------------------- | ------------ | -------------------------------------------- |
| Old English (Anglo-Saxon)  | 449–1066     | Beowulf, Caedmon, Bede                       |
| Middle English             | 1066–1500    | Chaucer, Langland, Malory                    |
| Renaissance / Elizabethan  | 1500–1660    | Shakespeare, Marlowe, Spenser, Bacon, Milton |
| Restoration / Neoclassical | 1660–1798    | Dryden, Pope, Swift, Defoe                   |
| Romantic                   | 1798–1832    | Wordsworth, Coleridge, Byron, Shelley, Keats |
| Victorian                  | 1837–1901    | Dickens, Hardy, Tennyson, Browning           |
| Modern                     | 1901–1945    | Eliot, Joyce, Woolf, Yeats, Lawrence         |

Sub-divisions: Elizabethan (1558–1603, reign of Elizabeth I), Jacobean (1603–1625), Caroline (1625–1649), Commonwealth/Puritan (1649–1660), Augustan (c.1700–1745), Age of Johnson (1745–1798).

## 2. Old and Middle English

- **Beowulf** (anonymous): oldest surviving English epic, written in Old English; hero Beowulf of the Geats fights Grendel, Grendel's mother and a dragon
- **Caedmon:** first English poet known by name
- **Bede:** _Ecclesiastical History of the English People_ (in Latin)
- **King Alfred:** _Anglo-Saxon Chronicle_

**Middle English**

- **Geoffrey Chaucer** (c.1343–1400): **Father of English Poetry**; _The Canterbury Tales_ (pilgrims travelling from the Tabard Inn, Southwark, to Canterbury, mostly in heroic couplets); _Troilus and Criseyde_; _The Book of the Duchess_; _The House of Fame_
- **William Langland:** _Piers Plowman_
- **John Gower:** _Confessio Amantis_
- **Gawain poet:** _Sir Gawain and the Green Knight_
- **Sir Thomas Malory:** _Le Morte d'Arthur_ (1485)
- **John Wycliffe:** first English translation of the Bible
- **William Caxton:** introduced the printing press to England (1476)

## 9. Literary Terms

| Term                 | Meaning / Example                                                                       |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Simile**           | Comparison using _like/as_: _brave as a lion_                                           |
| **Metaphor**         | Direct comparison: _He is a lion._                                                      |
| **Personification**  | Human traits to non-human things: _The wind whispered._                                 |
| **Hyperbole**        | Exaggeration: _I have told you a million times._                                        |
| **Irony**            | Meaning opposite to what is said, or events opposite to expectations                    |
| **Oxymoron**         | Contradictory words together: _bitter sweet, deafening silence_                         |
| **Paradox**          | Seemingly contradictory but true statement: _The child is father of the man._           |
| **Alliteration**     | Repeating initial consonant sounds: _Peter Piper picked..._                             |
| **Onomatopoeia**     | Sound imitation: _buzz, hiss, bang_                                                     |
| **Metonymy**         | Substituting a related word: _the Crown_ for the king                                   |
| **Synecdoche**       | Part for whole: _hands_ for workers                                                     |
| **Euphemism**        | Mild word for harsh one: _passed away_                                                  |
| **Allusion**         | Reference to a known person/event/work                                                  |
| **Pun**              | Wordplay using double meaning                                                           |
| **Apostrophe**       | Addressing an absent person or thing: _O Death, where is thy sting?_                    |
| **Litotes**          | Understatement by negation: _not bad_                                                   |
| **Epigram**          | A short witty saying                                                                    |
| **Pathetic fallacy** | Giving human feelings to nature                                                         |
| **Allegory**         | A story with a hidden moral/political meaning (_Animal Farm_, _The Pilgrim's Progress_) |
| **Satire**           | Ridiculing vice/folly (_Gulliver's Travels_)                                            |

### Poetic Forms

- **Sonnet:** 14 lines; **Petrarchan** (octave abbaabba + sestet), **Shakespearean** (3 quatrains + couplet: abab cdcd efef gg), **Spenserian** (abab bcbc cdcd ee)
- **Ode:** lofty lyric (Keats, Shelley)
- **Elegy:** poem of mourning (_Lycidas_, _Adonais_, Gray's _Elegy_)
- **Epic:** long narrative about heroes (_Paradise Lost_, _Beowulf_)
- **Mock-epic:** treats a trivial theme in epic style (_The Rape of the Lock_)
- **Ballad:** narrative song in quatrains
- **Pastoral:** rural life (Spenser's _Shepheardes Calender_)
- **Dramatic monologue:** single speaker addresses a listener (Browning's "My Last Duchess")
- **Blank verse:** unrhymed iambic pentameter (Marlowe, Shakespeare, Milton)
- **Heroic couplet:** rhymed iambic pentameter pairs (Chaucer, Dryden, Pope)
- **Free verse:** no fixed metre or rhyme (Whitman, Eliot)

### Drama

- **Tragedy:** serious play ending in the hero's downfall
- **Comedy:** light play with a happy ending
- **Tragicomedy, Farce, Comedy of manners, Theatre of the absurd**
- **Aristotle's _Poetics_:** key terms: _catharsis_ (purging pity and fear), _hamartia_ (tragic flaw), _peripeteia_ (reversal), _anagnorisis_ (recognition)
- **Three unities:** time, place, action
- **Soliloquy** (character speaks thoughts alone) vs **Aside** (brief remark to the audience)

### Fiction

- **Bildungsroman:** novel of growth (_David Copperfield_, _Great Expectations_)
- **Picaresque:** adventures of a rogue (_Tom Jones_, _Moll Flanders_)
- **Epistolary:** told through letters (_Pamela_)
- **Gothic:** mystery, horror (_Frankenstein_, _Wuthering Heights_)
- **Stream of consciousness:** Joyce, Woolf
- **Historical novel:** Scott
- **Dystopia:** Orwell, Huxley

## Study Plan

- Learn period by period, one major writer at a time (name → dates → works → epithet).
- Revise "Who wrote it?", epithets and literary terms daily; most MCQs come from these.
- For written questions, practise writing a short note on Shakespeare, Milton, Wordsworth, Keats, Dickens, Hardy and T. S. Eliot (life, major works, features, contribution).
